From an extreme worm’s-eye view, the camera lies flush against wet asphalt at a quiet suburban crosswalk, looking almost vertically upward. Looming overhead and blotting out the sky is an impossible machine: a monolithic, horizontal slab of carved limestone shaped like a giant rotary telephone dial. 

Instead of tires, the vehicle glides on four titanic, perfectly smooth obsidian spheres that compress the wet road, reflecting distorted telephone poles and streetlamps in their glossy black surfaces. Protruding from the dark undercarriage is a dense, rhythmic forest of hundreds of full-sized wooden violin bows, all sawing feverishly against the pavement in synchronized strokes to drive the colossal mass forward. 

The machine has no cockpit, engine block, or driver. Within the ten circular finger-holes on top of the limestone slab rest ten massive, unblinking human corneas, moist and gazing straight up into the blank sky. 

Rendered in crisp, high-contrast black ink, meticulous stippling defines the gritty asphalt and puddle ripples in the immediate foreground, while sharp cross-hatching sculpts the heavy stone underbelly. In the lower periphery, an ordinary stop sign and sagging utility wires frame the crossing, anchoring the unsettling, silent absurdity of the transit.
